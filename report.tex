\section{Basic Technique (~ lines 695 - 800)}
Our basic technique is a search algorithm over orders to find the best moves for our units. It finds each possible move for each unit, and then performs hill climbing, starting with all units holding 
and repeatedly changing a unit's order if it improves the score.
That score is heuristically calculated based on the probability of it succeeding (actually taking a space), and the value of that space, increased based on proximity to a supply centre and then more so for holding or taking a supply centre.
We chose this algorithm as a starting point because we wanted to consider our units as a joint force, instead of greedily sending them towards supply centres in attacks that could fail or moves that could weaken our defensive position. 
The hill climbing search method is an efficient way of achieving this, and is also very good at working within a time limit as we can stop whenever and have a decent solution.

On average in scenario 1, this agent ended the game with 11.5 supply centres (varying by 0.65), and a 23.8\% rate of victory 
Curiously, in scenario 2, it actually did better, achieving 13.24 supply centres (varying by 0.39), and a 45.2\% rate of victory.
In our estimates for scenario 3, which became more accurate later as we had more methods to experiment on, it had 12.86 supply centres (varying by 0.89) and a 47.6\% winrate.

To us, this indicated that the agent was able to capitalise well on the mistakes of some of the other agents that it avoided making, leading to it doing better in scenario 2 and 3. 
Maybe this is because these agents left some supply centres undefended, but in scenario 1 the static agent always has one unit holding their supply centre which must have made it harder for our agent. 
Overall, we think this should be a solvable problem, and the agent should be able to get much better results in scenario 1. Either way, the results from scenario 2 are very promising about our strategy.

\section{New Techniques}

\underline{1: Tracking enemy behaviour to identify agent tendencies (~lines 480-600)}

Our first improvement was directly intended to solve the problem we ran into above, where we are better against some agents than others. If we could determine which type opposing agents are, then we can adjust our strategies to 
more reliably work against them (just like in real life games). 

It could be quite easy to determine which agents are static, random, attitude, and greedy from scenarios 1 and 2 as we have the code for those, but this would generalise very poorly against both the hidden agent and our peers' agents. 
Instead, we decided to try and estimate opposing agents as either greedy (moving optimally towards supply centres), static (always holding), erratic (relatively random or attitude based moves), and strong, an estimate for agents that move
purposefully, but not predictably. This is defined by agents that are greedy 40-85\% of the time, which was found empirically through testing agents, as one of our own strong planning agents matched this schema (with an earlier agent of ours
being greedy 64\% of the time).
All of these checks are typically less expensive than our basic search algorithm, so we can afford to do them for each opposing agent. 

We can use these estimates of our opponents to change the expected probability of their moves, and now we can suggest that a particularly strong agent is very likely to defend optimally, so attacking with insufficient support is a poor idea, 
while static agents will never provide support so we can attack them where the previous agent would be too cautious. Additionally, they would never attack us, which removed the need to defend against them. 
This agent improved greatly in scenario 1, achieving a consistent 18 supply centres and jumping to an 100\% winrate. However, it achieved relatively similarly in supply centres and winrate to the previous agent in scenario 2, 
perhaps because we were already performing about as well against opposing agents, but this also could be due to misclassification of the random or attitude agents, and the element of randomness in scenario 2.
Notably, our first iteration of this strategy did not consider the strong opponent category, and our simulations of scenario 3, where we used one of our own planning bots as a stand-in for the hidden agent, became worse by -1.23 supply centres
on average (with an average count of 9.95 centres). Because the stronger bot did not cleanly fit into being static or greedy, it was classified as erratic, leading our bot to make inaccurate predictions assuming randomness from the bot. The addition of the strong agent type rectified this, bringing back the performance in this S3 simulation to 11.42 supply centres on average.

\underline{2: Top-k hill climb selection (in hill climb, saved in lines 800-819 and survivors checked in 1042-1065)}

Initially, the search process was to run separate restarting hill climbs, and then take the best orders found. As checking the actual likelihood of each move succeeding, especially against now measured opponents, must be done many times in hill climbing, we required a
fast heuristic to be able to calculate many prospective moves. However, a quickly computed heuristic can be inaccurate, especially in choosing the best from a set of good plans.
It could easily have been the case that one of the runner-up approaches would have fared much better, but was lost as we only took heuristically best. The solution is a top-k hill climb selection (we landed on k=6 initially, increasing to 8 for the final agent) by keeping the six highest plans, and then simulating the game one turn ahead with opponent moves sampled from the opponent's agent type (as described in Technique 1) through a Monte Carlo simulation, running by giving each opponent's unit a random move with probabilities weighted based on
their agent type. These sets of opponent moves (about 120 simulations per turn) are played against our six plans, and each simulation is heuristically graded based on owned supply centres, proximity to supply centres, and unit count. The plan with the highest average score in these simulations was chosen. 

This improvement held our success in scenario 1, and improved our performance in scenario 2, pushing us up only slightly in supply centres to around 15 but improving our winrate substantially to around 60\%, to be expected as a way of capitalising 
more on our opponent behaviour tracking. 

\underline{3: Genetic Algorithm (~lines 970 - 1050)}

A genetic algorithm was implemented to further improve the hill climbing search. Genetic algorithms work by creating a population of solutions, and iteratively selecting the best solutions (computed using a heuristic called a fitness function)
to combine with each other to create new solutions, with some random mutations which can introduce novel solutions. Solutions are combined by mixing 'genes', elements of the solution. We chose to implement a genetic algorithm, as we observed that of the plans created by hill climbing, some could have good moves
for some units but not others, and believed combining these plans could create stronger ones, which a genetic algorithm was a good fit for, with the random mutations also possibly introducing moves not considered by hill climbing, and there being
an easy way to implement genes as the orders for each unit.

For our fitness function, we used an average of simulated turns, calculated again based on owned supply centres, proximity to supply centres, and unit count, but also simulating until the end of Fall even in Spring, so that supply centre ownership changes are better accounted for even on the turns they do not change.

For the agent, we started with a population of approximately 20 plans, with the 8 plans from hill climbing, as well as random variations of going to the nearest supply centre and convoy plans that require multiple units ordered together.
Of those plans, the best 8 are kept as parents, and new plans are bred by combining genes (individual unit orders) until there are 16 (including the parents). Each unit also has a 10\% chance to 
'mutate' by randomly changing to a new order chosen from its gene pool, any move from one of the starting plans, or hold.

To prevent newer plans generated near the end from being chosen due to luck (as they may only run a few simulations, which has much higher variance than the large number run by older plans), a final race using new simulations is performed with the best 5 plans at the end.
The best plan from this final race is chosen as the final plan.

This genetic algorithm replaced the simpler simulation from the previous hill climbing technique, retaining elements of the hill climbing with the genetic algorithm added as the simulation layer.

At first, the final race was not performed. Without it, the bot did not do noticeably different than the top-k hill climbing, capturing +0.07 supply centres, with a 0.19 standard error, on average compared to top-k hill climbing,
which meant the two strategies performed extremely similarly.
Without the race, S2 had an average supply centre count of 16.98, varying by 0.22, and 15.51, varying by 0.33 in our estimation of S3. 
After adding the final race, the bot had a notable average improvement of +0.58 with a standard error of 0.18 compared to the previous strategy.
After the race, S2 had an average supply centre count of 17.17 in S2, varying by 0.21, and 16.70, varying by 0.24 in S3. The winrate jumps to around 90\% in S2 and 85\% in S3 are very promising for the results of this algorithm.

The largest gains were in S3, where the strong opponent type existed. The genetic algorithm reduced the number of bad moves made, which reduced the bot's chances of collapsing and losing in winnable scenarios.
